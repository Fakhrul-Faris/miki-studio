## Sources

- **The 5× claim has no known study behind it.** Keiningham, Vavra, Aksoy & Wallard, *Loyalty Myths* (Wiley, 2005),
  Myth #8, excerpt published by Ipsos Loyalty ([PDF](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/publication/2003-08/Ipsos_Loyalty_Myth_8_Excerpt.pdf)):
  "Although it is difficult to determine the exact origins of this platitude, the earliest sources that we can find
  attribute it to research conducted by the Technical Assistance Research Project (TARP) in Washington, D.C. in the late
  1980s." … "In 1990, … Christopher Hart and Harvard professors James Heskett and W. Earl Sasser, Jr. lent the statement
  further credibility in their Harvard Business Review article 'The Profitable Art of Service Recovery.'" The authors also
  admit: "We, too, have published prior works repeating this fallacy."
  ⚠ We don't have the 1990 service-recovery article's own wording; the script says only that HBR "printed it", which the
  Ipsos excerpt supports. "No study behind it" = no study with a sample and method has been found (Ipsos: "difficult to
  determine the exact origins"; [Iasbeck](https://icaroiasbeck.com/en/conteudos/retencao-de-clientes/): "I found no
  study with a sample and method that supports this ratio").
- **2014: "five to 25 times", no study named.** Amy Gallo, "The Value of Keeping the Right Customers", HBR, 29 Oct 2014
  ([hbr.org](https://hbr.org/2014/10/the-value-of-keeping-the-right-customers)): "Depending on which study you believe,
  and what industry you're in, acquiring a new customer is anywhere from five to 25 times more expensive than retaining
  an existing one."
- **The companion stat grew too (caption/pinned comment only, not in the voice):** Reichheld & Sasser, "Zero Defections:
  Quality Comes to Services", HBR Sep–Oct 1990, 68(5): 105–111 — reducing defections by 5% "generated 85% more profits in
  one bank's branch system, 50% more in an insurance brokerage, and 30% more in an auto-service chain" (case examples,
  no published method). The "25% to 95%" range appears later (Reichheld & Schefter, "E-Loyalty", HBR 2000; Reichheld,
  *Loyalty Rules!*, 2001) and is what the 2014 HBR piece quotes.
- **The real math (L05), same 1990 article:** "In credit cards, for example, companies spend an average of $51 to
  recruit a customer and set up the new account." "If … the credit card customer leaves after the first year, the
  company takes a $21 loss." Cutting the defection rate from 20% to 10% means "the value of that customer more than
  doubles — jumping from $134 to $300." (Quoted via [managementquotes](https://managementquotes.wordpress.com/2023/12/31/zero-defections-quality-comes-to-services-quotes-from-frederick-f-reichheld-and-w-earl-sasser-jr/)
  and the search summaries; ⚠ check against the HBR original's exhibit if a copy is reachable before voice.)
