## CREATIVE_INTENT
Lighting the window itself (sticking the key light directly behind it, aimed straight through) blows the whole window
out and flattens the scene — "guns blazing all the level." Instead, light the gap in the curtains/sheers — place the
source off to the side of the window, shining across, so the window area goes a controlled soft level while a real
practical, motivated patch of light (and a little bounce) reaches the subject's face without flattening the room.

## STE_PROCEDURE
1. If a window/opening is visible in frame, don't aim the key straight through its centre.
2. Place the light off to the side of the window (either side), shooting across rather than straight through.
3. Avoid lighting the curtain/sheer material itself full-on; aim for the gap or the space just past it.
4. Let the result bounce a little fill onto the subject rather than flooding the window area with full level.
5. Check the window reads as a believable light source without becoming the brightest, flattest thing in frame.

## AGENT_EXECUTION
Frame brief light field: note the window as a "motivated but not directly lit" source — the actual light sits beside
it, aimed across. ref_<shot>.md: mark the window's own brightness as controlled/secondary, not the hottest point in
frame.

### Worked example
Topic: a tailor shop's single shopfront window as the only daylight source in an interior beat.
BEFORE: a light rigged directly behind the window, aimed straight through — the window blows out white, the interior
reads flat.
AFTER: the light moved beside the window frame, shining across past it — the window itself stays a soft, readable
rectangle of daylight, while the bounce gives the sewing machine on the counter a believable soft edge-light.

### Check
- Is the light placed beside the window (or opening), not aimed straight through its centre?
- Does the window stay a controlled, readable brightness rather than blowing out?
- Does the subject get real dimensional light from the bounce/gap, not just flat window glow?
